# Planning Optimiser — Next Engineering Phase

## Objective

The current national model is good enough to remain as the baseline planning-risk engine.

Do **not** prioritise further marginal improvements to its overall AUC.

The next objective is to build a separate **structured planning optimiser model** that can respond meaningfully and defensibly to variables a developer can actually change.

The target product output is eventually:

> Current scheme: 61% modelled grant probability
> Increase upper-floor setback from 0.5 m to 2.5 m
> New modelled probability: 74%
> Primary reasons: X, Y, Z
> Supported by matched precedents and comparable redesign sequences

We are not there yet.

The current model does not use height, setbacks, density, parking, open space or most other design variables, so these cannot currently drive optimiser recommendations.

---

# 1. Preserve the existing v3 model

Treat v3 as the frozen baseline risk model.

Current holdout:

* Train: 203,931 cases through 2024
* Future test: 15,221 cases in 2025
* ROC-AUC: 0.7989
* PR-AUC: 0.4202
* Calibrated Brier: 0.0849
* Calibrated log loss: 0.2908
* Strong probability calibration across deciles

Do not replace or materially refactor v3 unless necessary.

Its role should become:

**general national planning risk prior**

rather than:

**scheme design optimiser**

---

# 2. Important current limitation

The existing model responds mainly to:

* planning authority
* application type/bucket
* year
* development-description text
* historical refusal rates
* location/site history
* comparable-case text aggregates

The following variables are captured but currently effectively unmodelled:

* maximum height
* number of storeys
* upper-floor setback
* front/rear/side setbacks
* density
* plot ratio
* site coverage
* parking
* bicycle parking
* open space
* separation distances
* access configuration

These are the variables the optimiser ultimately needs to manipulate.

Therefore:

**Do not expose scenario probability movements caused solely by text wording as planning recommendations.**

Example:

Changing:

“42 apartments”

to:

“42 apartments with communal open space, parking and landscaping”

moves the model substantially.

That is predictive behaviour, but it is not evidence that adding those design features causes a lower probability.

Free-text manipulation must never become the optimiser.

# 4. Data quality matters more than raw quantity

Do not fill missing structured variables with speculative LLM guesses.

Use three confidence levels:

* **verified** — manually checked or directly structured
* **high-confidence extracted**
* **unknown**

Unknown is preferable to fabricated precision.

Produce coverage reports for every field.

Example:

| Field               | Non-null | High confidence |
| ------------------- | -------: | --------------: |
| Units               |      92% |             91% |
| Storeys             |      78% |             71% |
| Height              |      54% |             47% |
| Parking             |      64% |             58% |
| Upper-floor setback |      12% |              8% |

This tells us which optimiser levers are actually ready.

# 5. Prioritise variables in this order

Extraction effort should focus first on:

1. units
2. storeys
3. maximum height
4. density
5. parking
6. upper-floor setback
7. separation distances
8. open space
9. site coverage / plot ratio
10. access arrangement

Height/storeys/units should be easiest and give the fastest proof that structured scenario modelling works.

Setbacks are strategically important but likely harder to extract reliably.

### Structured design variables

* units

# 7. Scenario sanity tests are mandatory

For each proposed optimiser variable, construct controlled scenario tests.

Example:

Same scheme, only height changes:

12 m
14 m
16 m
18 m
20 m

Record predicted probability.

Repeat for:

* units
* density
* storeys
* parking
* setbacks
* separation

Flag models where effects are:

* implausibly discontinuous
* highly unstable
* dominated by missingness
* reversed without evidence
* extremely sensitive to tiny changes

The optimiser model must behave sensibly under perturbation before it is exposed in the UI.

---

# 10. Do not infer causality from SHAP

The optimiser must never say:

> Height contributes −12 percentage points, therefore reducing height adds +12 points.

SHAP explains model prediction, not causal effect.

Instead separate:

### Model explanation

“Height is associated with elevated refusal risk in this prediction.”

### Scenario result

“Changing height from 18 m to 15 m changes the modelled probability from 58% to 67%.”

### Evidence

“Comparable applications and revision pairs involving lower height show better outcomes in similar contexts.”

Only after all three align should the UI recommend the intervention strongly.

---

# 11. Build evidence strength for each recommendation

Every future optimiser recommendation should receive an evidence grade.

Example:

## Strong

* model scenario moves materially
* enough matched precedent
* multiple relevant revision pairs
* relevant refusal reason
* relevant policy/officer evidence

## Moderate

* model scenario moves
* some precedent
* limited revision-pair evidence

## Weak

* model movement only
* little direct precedent
* no revision-pair support

The UI should surface this.

Example:

> Increase upper-floor setback to 2.5 m
> Modelled probability: 61% → 74%
> Evidence strength: **Moderate**

---

# 12. Comparable retrieval needs a proper benchmark

Current comparable retrieval has no formal relevance benchmark.

Create one.

Build a manually labelled evaluation set of at least:

**100–300 query applications**

For each query, label:

* highly relevant comparable
* relevant
* weak
* irrelevant

Evaluate:

* Precision@5
* Precision@10
* NDCG@10
* recall of known same-type precedents

The optimiser will rely heavily on comparables, so relevance quality must become measurable.

---

# 13. Separate three probability engines

Architect toward three related outputs:

### A. Baseline national risk

Existing v3.

### B. Structured scheme probability

New structured model.

### C. Ultimate permission probability

Later model including appeals.

Do not merge these into one opaque score.

Potential future UI:

* Baseline contextual grant probability: 71%
* Structured scheme grant probability: 63%
* Ultimate permission probability: 76%

# 14. First practical optimiser milestone

Do not wait for setback extraction to be perfect.

The first milestone should work with 3–4 strong structured variables.

Example:

User enters:

* 42 units
* 6 storeys
* 20 m height
* 72 units/ha

System predicts:

**Grant probability: 58%**

Then user tests:

### Scenario A

5 storeys
17 m

**66%**

### Scenario B

4 storeys
14 m

**74%**

The goal is simply to prove that:

1. structured variables affect probability;
2. movements are stable and sensible;
3. similar historical schemes support the direction;
4. results are properly caveated as modelled scenarios.

# 16. Definition of success for this phase

Do not judge success primarily by beating v3's 0.799 AUC.

This phase succeeds when we can produce several examples like:

> A 52-unit, six-storey residential proposal receives a 59% modelled grant probability.
>
> Holding contextual variables constant:
>
> * reducing to five storeys → 66%
> * reducing height from 20 m to 17 m → 69%
> * reducing units from 52 to 46 → 71%
>
> The height/storey intervention is supported by six highly relevant precedents and three comparable refused → revised → granted project histories.
>
> Evidence strength: Moderate.

That is much closer to the eventual product than getting national AUC from 0.799 to 0.805.
