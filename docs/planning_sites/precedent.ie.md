### Why Precedent exists

I built this the slow way. Reading council decision documents one at a time to find out what planners in each county actually object to, until there were 16,834 of them and it had become a database rather than a habit.

Which is why the rule here is that nothing gets invented. Every figure on this page comes from a query you could run yourself, rounded down rather than up. Where the data cannot answer something — who owns a site, what it last sold for — Precedent says so instead of estimating. A confident wrong answer about a planning constraint is worse than no answer, because someone acts on it.

It is early, and the gaps listed above are the real list rather than a modest one. If you work with Irish land and something you need is not in here, tell me — that is most of how the roadmap gets written.

### Frequently asked questions

#### What is Precedent?

A land sourcing and planning data platform for Ireland. It holds every planning decision in the State — 203,449 applications across all 31 planning authorities — alongside national land-use zoning, flood extents, protected structures, ecological designations, subsoil mapping and Census 2022 demographics, so a site can be screened against all of them at once instead of one viewer at a time.

#### Who is it for?

Developers and land sourcers judging whether a site is worth bidding on; planners, architects and consultants who need to know what a council has objected to nearby; renewable energy developers screening for the constraints that actually refuse schemes; and land agents who need a holding’s planning history evidenced before they advertise it.

#### What data is it built on?

Official public registries only. The national planning applications register (203,449 applications, 8,377 of them with a linked appeal to An Coimisiún Pleanála, formerly An Bord Pleanála), council decision documents read for the reasoning behind 16,834 refusals, GeoHive GZT land-use zoning, OPW/CFRAM flood extents, NPWS ecological sites, local authority Records of Protected Structures, Geological Survey Ireland subsoil mapping, each authority’s published Section 48 contribution scheme, Tailte Éireann’s Land Registry titles, ESB Networks’ grid capacity heatmap and the CSO’s farmland prices — plus OpenStreetMap’s mapped solar farms, which is volunteer-mapped rather than official. Nothing is scraped from property listings and nothing is estimated.

#### What does it cost?

The platform is €195 a month, with a minimum term of 12 months. There is no self-serve checkout yet — billing is still being built, so access is arranged and invoiced directly until it is ready. You can check the data before you decide: there is a public page for every one of the 203,449 applications on the register, carrying the council's own reasoning where it was refused.

#### How do I get access?

Book a demo and say what you work on. It is a short call on real sites in the county you work in, so you can judge it on your own ground. If it is useful, we set up your account and you sign in with your email — no password.

#### How does it help me find and assess a site?

Two ways in. If you are looking for land, the sourcing tool searches either the State’s Residential Zoned Land Tax map — narrowed by county or a drawn area, site size, what is built on it and how close the nearest planning application is — or every field in the State — for strategic land on a town’s edge, or solar, battery and data centre land near spare grid capacity. Stack it with permissions nobody has started, refusals a better scheme could answer, and the constraints you want to keep clear of. If you already have a site, click it: zoning, flood, ecology, protected structures, subsoil and the planning history around it come back together, with the council’s reasoning on every nearby refusal. Either way the site card gives you the coordinates to look up the owner on landdirect.ie.

#### What is it missing?

Owner names and site-level sale prices are not in it — Land Registry title boundaries are, but not who holds them, and house and farmland prices are medians for an area rather than for one site. Grid capacity is ESB Networks’ published heatmap, not a connection offer. The planning register we hold begins in 2021, so "no application here" means none in about five years, not never developed. The zoning layer covers 27 of the 31 authorities — Donegal and Wicklow are absent. Protected structures are loaded for 9 of the 31 (Dublin City, Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown, Fingal, South Dublin, Cork City, Galway City, Kildare, Roscommon and Wicklow), so elsewhere the map cannot tell you a building is protected. Development levy rates cover 12 authorities, on the public rates guide rather than in the dashboard. We would rather you knew that now than found it later.

#### Will this replace planning consultants?

No. Precedent does the desk research — pulling together what is already public and scattered across 31 council systems — so the judgement calls start from a complete picture. Reading the constraints is the easy half; arguing a scheme through a planning authority is not, and that stays with the professionals.

# Page 3: Why Limerick Refuses Planning Applications | Precedent.ie

Source URL: https://precedent.ie/blog/understanding_refusal_risks_in_limerick

Share

There is a version of this article that opens with a confident claim about refusals being up X% and planners getting stricter. It is easy to write and impossible to check.

Here is the checkable version instead. Every refused application on the Limerick planning register that we have been able to read the decision document for — 461 of them, as of 4 September 2026 — grouped by the reason the council actually wrote down.

The count grows as more decision documents are read, so it is stated with a date rather than as a running total: the figures below are the 461 refusals on record on that date, not a number that silently changes underneath the percentages it produced.

### What the register holds

- 6,650 Limerick planning applications on record

- 499 of those refused

- 461 with the reasoning extracted from the council's own decision document

- Decisions from June 2021 to July 2026

Refusals per year, for what it is worth: 23 (2021, partial), 85, 83, 101, 114, and 93 so far in 2026. The application base grew over the same period, so this is a count, not a rate — it does not show councils getting stricter, and anyone telling you it does needs to show you the denominator.

### The ranking

A refusal usually cites more than one ground, so these are the share of refusals in which each theme appears. They do not sum to 100.

Reason cited Share of Limerick refusals

Traffic & road safety 44%

Zoning & development plan conflict 40%

Visual & landscape impact 33%

Residential amenity 26%

Ecology & heritage 25%

Rural housing need 21%

Wastewater & water 19%

Density, scale & standards 18%

Flood risk 9%

Traffic is the single most common ground for refusal in Limerick, and it is not close. Sightlines at an entrance, an access onto a national or regional road, parking provision, intensification of an existing junction. Nearly one refusal in two turns on it somewhere.

That is not what the sector talks about. The conversation is usually density, design and objections. The decisions are about the entrance.

### What that looks like in the councils' own words

"The development would endanger public safety by reason of traffic hazard and is contrary to national policy regarding development on national roads and the protection of residential amenities."
— ref 2660588, July 2026

"The application was refused due to significant concerns regarding traffic safety at the proposed entrance and the environmental suitability of the site for a private wastewater treatment system."
— ref 2660411, July 2026

Note the second one. Traffic and wastewater arrive together constantly in rural Limerick applications — a site with a difficult entrance is often also a site relying on a private treatment system, and the two objections compound. Wastewater alone is cited in 19% of refusals; it is rarely the only thing wrong.

"The proposal fails to meet rural housing eligibility criteria and presents significant concerns regarding traffic safety and environmental impact."
— ref 2660597, July 2026

Rural housing need appears in 21%. Where it appears it is usually the operative reason — the eligibility test is a threshold question, and failing it is not something the rest of the application can compensate for.

### Two things worth taking from this

The entrance deserves the same attention as the elevation. On these numbers, sightlines and access are the most likely single thing to sink a Limerick application, and they are cheap to establish early relative to what a refused application costs.

Check what was refused near your site, not what was refused generally. A county-level ranking tells you where to look. It does not tell you what the planner dealing with your area has objected to on the road you are on, which is a different and more useful question.

### About these numbers

Every figure above comes from the Limerick rows of the Irish planning register and the councils' own published decision documents. The grouping was measured over all 461 refusals rather than assumed in advance, and the keyword rules behind each theme are the same ones used across the rest of the corpus.

The register we work from begins in 2021 for Limerick, so this is a five-year picture, not an all-time one. Refusals whose decision document could not be retrieved are not counted — 38 of the 499.

Precedent.ie holds every Irish planning decision and the reasoning behind the refusals. Every application on the register has a public page with the council's own reasoning on it.

# Page 4: Where Ireland's Spare Grid Capacity Is | Precedent.ie

Source URL: https://precedent.ie/blog/where-irelands-spare-grid-capacity-is

Share

ESB Networks publishes a heatmap of its substations and how much spare capacity each one has for new generation. It answers a question about substations.

A solar or battery developer is asking a different question: which fields could use that capacity? To answer it you need the substations, every field in the State, the flood maps, the designated sites and the building outlines, all at once. No free viewer joins them, so we did.

### What this is based on

- ESB Networks' availability capacity heatmap , as compiled on 12 December 2025. We counted the medium-voltage, 38 kV and 110 kV substations, the ones a generation project connects to.

- Every Land Registry title in the State : 3,217,764 freehold and leasehold titles from Tailte Éireann.

- A field counts here if it is 2 hectares or more , within 2 km of a substation with at least 5 MW of spare generation capacity , clear of mapped flood extents and designated sites (SAC, SPA, NHA and proposed NHA), has buildings on under 2.5% of its land , and is not already a solar farm (mapped in OpenStreetMap, or with a solar farm planning application inside it).

That gives 13,791 fields covering 113,454 hectares .

### The headline: most of the capacity is not where the fields are

453 medium- and high-voltage substations show spare generation capacity. 310 have at least 5 MW of it that is usable. Dublin has 117 of those, more than a third.

Cork has the most qualifying fields, and Carlow has none at all.

Substations are counted in the county they stand in; fields are counted in their own county, so a field can qualify on a substation across the border. Three substations fall outside every council boundary, and one field has no county on the Land Registry, which is why the columns sum to 307 and 13,790.

### Three things that stand out

1. A number on a map is not always capacity you can use. 31 of the 453 substations showing spare generation capacity are constrained further up the network, so that headroom cannot actually be reached. 19 of them show 5 MW or more. Kildare has five of those and Kerry four. We leave all of them out.

2. Carlow has no qualifying land at this threshold. No substation in the county shows 5 MW of usable spare generation capacity, and no Carlow field is within 2 km of one across the county line. At a lower threshold or a longer distance, the answer changes.

3. The biggest county for land is not the biggest county for substations. Dublin has almost three times as many qualifying substations as Cork, but fewer qualifying fields. Substations cluster where demand is; open land does not.

### What this does not tell you

- It is not a connection offer. The heatmap is ESB Networks' indication of headroom. Only a connection application tells you what a site can connect, and on what terms. It also says nothing about projects already queued for the same capacity.

- 38 kV and 110 kV substation positions are indicative. Against OpenStreetMap's mapping of the same stations they sit a median of about 800 m out, which is why we use a 2 km radius rather than a tight one.

- A database cannot tell a field from forestry. In a photo check of 100 random fields with few or no buildings on them, 70 were open land. Most of the rest were forestry, road verges and strips of land around housing estates. Look at the imagery before acting on a field.

- The heatmap changes. ESB republish it quarterly, and capacity is taken up as projects connect.

### A note on method

Each field's distance was measured from its boundary to the substation, in PostGIS, and the largest usable headroom within 500 m, 1 km, 2 km and 5 km was stored for every title of half a hectare or more. Flood extents are the OPW's mapping. Designated sites are the National Parks and Wildlife Service's. Building footprints are Tailte Éireann's; a building counts toward a field only when at least half of it is inside the boundary. "Usable" means the substation's headroom is not constrained further up the network, as ESB mark it.

# Page 5: Why Irish Renewable Projects Get Refused | Precedent.ie

Source URL: https://precedent.ie/blog/why-irish-renewable-energy-applications-get-refused

Share

A refused planning application for a utility-scale renewable project is an expensive thing. The site work, the ecological surveys, the EIAR, the grid application — largely sunk by the time the council writes back.

The reasons councils give are public. They are also scattered across 31 separate planning systems, published as scanned PDFs and portal schedules that nobody reads across.

So we read them. Every refused wind, solar and battery storage application on the Irish planning register, and grouped by the reason the decision maker actually wrote down.

### What this is based on

- 88 refused schemes , decided between 21 April 2021 and 13 July 2026

- 25 of the 31 planning authorities

- 40 wind, 38 solar, 18 battery storage (eight schemes are solar and storage, so the technology counts overlap)

- Every one read from the council's own decision document — the schedule of refusal reasons, or the planner's report

Two things this is not . It is not every renewable refusal ever: the register we work from runs from 2021, so an older refusal is not in it. And it is not a legal opinion — it is what the councils said, counted.

A refusal almost always cites several grounds. The percentages below are the share of schemes where a theme appears at all, so they do not sum to 100.

### The headline: ecology decides more cases than landscape

Reason cited Share of refusals

Ecology & Natura 2000 61%

Development plan / designation conflict 51%

Landscape & visual impact 50%

Traffic & road safety 40%

Insufficient information or assessment 36%

Residential amenity & noise 33%

Peat, ground & water 30%

Archaeology & heritage 19%

Grid & infrastructure 10%

The industry conversation about renewables planning in Ireland is mostly about landscape and local objection. The decisions are mostly about habitats . Appropriate Assessment, Annex I habitats, SAC and SPA integrity, and named species — Hen Harrier, Golden Plover — turn up in nearly two out of three refusals.

The second surprise is how often the decision was effectively made before the application was drawn: 51% cite a conflict with the development plan or a designation. That is a siting decision, not a design one.

### Wind: the designation is the decision

40 refusals.

Ecology & Natura 2000 70%

Development plan / designation conflict 55%

Landscape & visual impact 55%

Peat, ground & water 38%

Residential amenity & noise 35%

Wind is the technology where where you are matters more than what you propose. County wind energy strategies carve each county into zones, and a scheme in the wrong zone starts from behind:

"The proposal materially contravenes County Development Plan objectives by seeking to extend the life of a wind farm in a 'Normally Discouraged' area without demonstrating exceptional circumstances…"
— Cork County Council, ref 25/5406, September 2025

"The site is located in an area designated as 'unsuitable' for wind energy in the Tipperary Renewable Energy Strategy…"
— Tipperary County Council, ref 2360763, November 2023

"…located outside the designated Wind Energy Strategy Area and proposes a new access onto a national secondary road (N52)…"
— Offaly County Council, ref 21464, September 2021

Note that these zone designations are not a national layer. Each council draws its own, some publish a shapefile, most bury it in a development plan appendix.

#### Peat is a wind-specific killer

Peat appears in nine refusals, and it fails schemes in two separate ways that are worth keeping apart.

As a stability risk:

"…significant concerns regarding peat stability and landslide risk , potential adverse impacts on the Boleybrack Mountain SAC and protected species, and an incomplete cumulative environmental impact assessment."
— Leitrim County Council, ref 2157, February 2022

"…the elevated risk of a major landslide event and subsequent environmental pollution."
— Leitrim County Council, ref 2460008

As a habitat in its own right:

"…the unacceptable loss of Annex I peatland habitats ."
— Cork County Council, ref 25/142, May 2025

"…the loss of Annex I and high-value peatland habitats, and premature due to the refusal of essential road infrastructure in the neighbouring Kerry jurisdiction."
— Cork County Council, ref 23/5145, February 2024

The subsoil class under a site is mapped nationally by Geological Survey Ireland and can be checked in seconds, before anything is spent. It is rarely the first check anyone runs.

Insufficient information or assessment 42%

Solar refusals look different from wind refusals. There is no equivalent of the wind energy strategy zoning, the landscape argument is weaker, and what fills the gap is unglamorous: access roads and missing paperwork .

Traffic and road safety is cited in 42% of solar refusals — construction traffic on narrow rural roads, sightlines at a field entrance that was never designed for it. And 42% turn on an assessment that was not submitted at all:

"The applicant failed to provide a comprehensive Archaeological Impact Assessment and test excavations to ensure the protection of nearby Recorded Monuments…"
— Carlow County Council, February 2026

"Failure to provide adequate technical assessments regarding noise impacts, traffic safety on narrow rural roads, and specific grid connection details ."
— Kildare County Council

Archaeology deserves a specific mention on solar: a ground-mounted array disturbs a wide, shallow footprint, which is exactly the profile that finds subsurface remains. It shows up far more often than the sector's risk registers suggest.

### Battery storage: judged like an industrial building

18 refusals.

Ecology & Natura 2000 61%

Landscape & visual impact 56%

Development plan / designation conflict 50%

Traffic & road safety 50%

Insufficient information or assessment 44%

The smallest sample here, so read it as a signal rather than a finding. But the pattern is consistent: BESS applications are assessed like a piece of industrial plant dropped into a rural setting, not like energy infrastructure. Landscape and access carry as much weight as they would for a warehouse.

"…insufficient information regarding ecological impacts on the River Barrow and River Nore SAC, inadequate assessment…"
— Laois County Council, December 2025

### What the pattern suggests

Three things fall out of reading all 88.

1. Most of the risk is fixed at site selection. Ecology, designation conflict and landscape — the top three — are properties of the location. They are settled the day the option is signed, not during design.

2. A large minority are self-inflicted. 36% cite an assessment that was missing or inadequate. That is not a planning judgement going against you; it is a submission that was incomplete.

3. Grid barely appears. Only 10% of refusals mention grid or connection issues. Grid capacity decides whether a project is viable . It is almost never what the council refuses on. Both questions have to be answered, and they are answered by different work.

The fields & farmland search covers every Land Registry title in the State, leaving out fields in flood extents or designated sites and fields that already hold solar, and the grid capacity filter narrows it to land near substations with spare generation headroom. ### A note on method

Each refusal was read from the council's own decision document and summarised into a structured record: the core reason, the development plan sections cited, and the decision maker's grounds. The grouping above was measured across the full set rather than assumed in advance — 1 of the 88 did not fit any theme.

Two schemes were excluded because the published document turned out not to be a decision notice, and one refusal on purely procedural grounds was excluded as well. Refusals later overturned on appeal are included, and flagged as such in the underlying records, because the council's reasoning is still the thing a next applicant has to answer.
